Ask most people where AI sits in their week and they describe the before. Help me write this email. Help me brainstorm this offer. Help me prep for this pitch.

All of that works. I do it every day. And it's still the smallest part of what AI can do for you.

The real leverage is on the other side of the meeting.

## The before is where everybody starts

The before is AI working on the world's information. Draft this, summarize that. It's useful, and it has a ceiling, because everyone has the same access to the same before. The person down the street asks for five subject lines and gets five subject lines that sound a lot like yours.

The before makes you faster. It rarely makes you better. Getting better takes feedback on what you actually did, and the before has never seen you do anything.

## The after is where the money is

You just walked out of a discovery call. Or a meeting with the grant people, the developers, the school board, whoever your version of that is. That conversation is the richest data you will touch all week. Most people let it evaporate on the drive home. The notes app gets two bullets, the follow-up email goes out, and the best intelligence of the week is gone.

The after looks like this. Take the transcript of that meeting, put it into Claude, and ask: How did I do? Grade me out of 10. What did I miss? Where did I talk when I should have asked a question?

Fair warning: Claude is harsh. I've been handed a 6. I keep a sentence ready for it: "I'm not trying to get a better grade. I'm trying to get better." You need critical feedback to improve, and it's the one thing the people around you rarely supply. A prospect leaves without saying why the call felt off. A team goes easy on the boss. The transcript has no such manners. Ask it a direct question and you'll hear what nobody else will say to your face.

Do that after every meeting and you've built a feedback loop most professionals never get: same-day, specific, tied to words you actually said. You get better faster.

## None of this works without the transcript

The after has one prerequisite. You have to capture the conversation.

My standard is simple. If you had twenty meetings last week, you should have twenty transcripts. Otter, Fathom, the recorder built into Zoom or Meet or Teams, your phone on the table for in-person. I care much less about which tool than about consistency. Pick one, use it every time, and ask first: "Mind if I record this? I want to make sure you get the notes." Most people say yes. If they say no, you turn it off.

Then store all of them, even the ones you have no plan for yet. A transcript sitting untouched in your inbox earns you nothing. The value shows up the moment you start asking it questions.

## The after feeds the before

Now watch it compound. Before your next meeting with that same person, hand Claude the last transcript and the emails since, and say: prep me. The prep that took fifteen or twenty minutes drops to five or ten, and it comes back specific. What they said they wanted, in their words. What you promised. Where the friction was. Somebody books time with you after six months of silence, and you walk in fresh on everything that mattered to them.

So the after of this meeting becomes the before of the next one. Everyone's before looks about the same. Your after is built from conversations only you were in, and that is an edge nobody can buy off a shelf.

## Do this today

After your next meeting, paste the transcript into Claude and ask three questions. Grade me out of 10. What did I miss? What's the one thing I should do differently next time? Ten minutes, and you'll learn things about yourself that years of meetings never told you.
