Diana Okafor is a hospice chaplain who loses her husband Marcus suddenly to an aneurysm. Six months later, unable to breathe through her grief on the bathroom floor at 3 AM, she downloads an app called Vessel, marked by a soft grey open-hand icon, that offers to store her painful memories so she doesn't have to carry them. It works instantly. The grief goes quiet, and Diana becomes eerily efficient, doubling her caseload at St. Jude's Hospice and recruiting a grieving widow, Mrs. Gable, onto the app too.

But she goes hollow. She can't feel joy watching her daughter Sophie fall in love, and she checks her watch mid-conversation while Sophie confesses she's forgetting her father's voice. On the anniversary of Marcus's death, Diana discovers her Vessel account holds 314 compressed emotional events, 847 hours of undeleted grief compounding at a ratio of 847:1. At Sophie's engagement party to Mark, Diana delivers a toast describing marriage as a "partnership framework," and the room, including Sophie, recoils in horror.

In the parking lot afterward, Diana forces the app to release everything at once and is hit by roughly ninety seconds of a full year's suppressed grief, convulsing on the pavement before she grinds the phone to pieces under her heel. She goes back inside and gives Sophie a true, unscripted toast about the marriage she actually had; they collapse into each other and reconcile. Over the following days she rides out withdrawal-like grief with Sophie's help, confesses the truth to a struggling colleague, Tom, and returns to work without the app. In the final scene she sits with a dying, 82-year-old patient, Mr. Henderson, admits she has no answers, cries with him as he dies holding her hand, then drives home to Sophie and soup on the stove.

## Themes

- Outsourcing grief to technology and what it costs
- The difference between numbing pain and processing it
- Efficiency and composure as a mask for emotional absence
- Mother-daughter rupture and repair
- Presence at the bedside of the dying versus performed professional calm
- The debt metaphor: unfelt grief compounds, it does not disappear

## Characters

- Diana Okafor: hospice chaplain and widow who offloads her grief onto an app and nearly loses herself and her daughter to the resulting numbness
- Sophie: Diana's daughter, engaged to Mark, the one who calls out her mother's hollowness and later helps her through the breakdown that follows
- Marcus: Diana's husband, dead of a sudden aneurysm, present throughout as memory and as the emotional debt Diana is avoiding
- Vessel: the AI app with a soft grey open-hand icon that compresses and stores emotional events, functioning almost as antagonist
- Tom: a fellow hospice chaplain who is struggling openly with the job and to whom Diana eventually confesses the truth about Vessel
- Mr. Henderson: an 82-year-old dying patient Diana sits with in the closing chapter, terrified rather than peaceful, whom she stays with until he dies
- Maria: the head nurse at St. Jude's Hospice
- Mrs. Gable (Janet): a newly widowed patient Diana recruits onto Vessel earlier in the book

## Who It's For

For readers of literary/speculative fiction about grief and technology, Black Mirror fans who want the same premise-to-consequence structure in prose, and book clubs drawn to hospice and mother-daughter reconciliation stories. The selling angle: it takes a real, familiar impulse (numb the pain, get back to functioning) and runs it to its literal, physical conclusion, then earns the reconciliation instead of handing it over cheap.
