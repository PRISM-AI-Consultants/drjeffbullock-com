Jordan, a hospital pharmacist, starts his brother Elias on Volexra, a new weekly injectable for Type 2 diabetes. The drug works. Elias's A1C drops from 9.4 to under 6, he loses weight, his blood pressure normalizes. But the change doesn't stop at his body. Elias stops arguing. He accepts a layoff without pushing for full severance, doesn't contest an eviction notice papered as a renovation pretext, moves from his apartment into his landlord Henderson's basement, and keeps sending money to Sarah, the ex-girlfriend who left him. He does underpaid labor for Henderson and calls all of it efficient.

Digging through hospital and manufacturer databases at night, Jordan finds the pattern isn't unique to Elias. Volexra produces near-total adherence and zero complaints specifically in low-income Black men aged 30 to 55, a subgroup the manufacturer's internal documents label a "Silence Signal" and market as a retention asset and an equity win. Notes on future indications mention correctional facilities and other "high-friction institutional settings." A pharmaceutical liaison, Elena Rostova, warns Jordan off the investigation by reframing his concern as burnout. An attorney, Caldwaller, tells him there's no case: Elias denies being harmed and won't serve as a plaintiff. Jordan models what pulling Elias off the drug would do to his health, housing, and job, and the model shows collapse. He deletes his evidence.

The book ends in October with Jordan visiting a thinner, calmer, more compliant Elias, who is now digitizing Henderson's property archives in exchange for a small rent break and weighing a second, low-paid night job to fill his remaining free time. Both brothers have settled into calling it stable.

## Themes

- Medicine as an instrument of social control disguised as care
- Racialized compliance marketed internally as an equity win
- The commodification of the easy patient
- Family loyalty as complicity
- Euphemism as a laundering mechanism (friction, silence signal, clinical calm, stabilization)
- A harm with no legal standing because the victim can't feel it anymore
- Survival adaptation versus selfhood

## Characters

- Jordan - narrator, hospital pharmacist and Elias's healthcare proxy, who finds the data and chooses silence
- Elias - Jordan's brother, a warehouse worker whose diabetes is cured by Volexra as his will to resist anything disappears
- Sarah Jenkins - Elias's ex-girlfriend, who keeps taking his money even after leaving him, and later marries someone else
- Mr. Henderson - Elias's elderly landlord, who evicts him under a pretextual renovation and then uses him for underpaid labor
- Elena Rostova, PharmD - the drug distributor's regional medical liaison, who steers Jordan away from his investigation
- Dr. Aris - Elias's endocrinologist, thrilled with his 'boring' labs
- Mr. Caldwaller - the attorney who tells Jordan he has no legal case because his own brother won't claim harm
- Volexra - the tirzepatide-analog GLP-1/GIP diabetes drug at the center of the book, later given softer, wellness-style packaging

- The Every by Dave Eggers (institutional euphemism as the real villain)

## Who It's For

For readers of literary dystopian and social thrillers who want the horror delivered in clinical language rather than spectacle. The strongest selling angle: it's a pharma-industry conspiracy told entirely in pharmacology, spreadsheets, and market-access decks, and the narrator's final move is not exposure but a documented, reasoned choice to bury what he found, which leaves the reader with no clean resolution to root for.
